Who Are We?

A reflection on the scientific and Qur’anic stories of human origins, and on how the question of where we come from turns into the question of what we are becoming.

I used to think the question “Who are we?” could be answered by choosing one story and rejecting the others. I spent years trying to understand where we fit in the universe, and why the answers always seem to split into competing stories. One story speaks the language of atoms, fossils, and cosmic time; another speaks of Adam, revelation, purpose, and divine wisdom. For a long time I felt forced to choose between them. Now I wonder whether the deeper question is not which one is true enough to survive, but what each one is actually trying to reveal about us.

A small disclaimer

Before we go any further, a small disclaimer: both the scientific and Islamic stories I mention here have many branches, debates, and details. Astronomers disagree about aspects of cosmology; Muslim scholars differ on how exactly to read some verses about creation and Adam. This article is neither an attempt to win a debate nor a full map of those disagreements. It is an attempt to step back, look at the broader picture, and an invitation for you to start your own journey into the details that matter most to you.

The scientific timeline

Before any scripture is read, the cosmos already seems to be speaking in its own language of time — the scientific lens takes us into a history measured not in generations, but in millions and billions of years by looking at fossils, stars, and genetic evidence. Current cosmology places the universe at about 13.8 billion years old,[6] beginning from an extremely hot and dense early state. As the universe cools and structures form, the story narrows from galaxies to planets, and from planets to traces of humanity emerging from stone and bone.

One of the most important of those traces was found at Jebel Irhoud[1] in Morocco, where fossils of early Homo sapiens pushed our species' history much deeper into the past than many had imagined.

The scientific story of Homo sapiens is not a story of simple “caveman” becoming modern people overnight. It is a long history of gradual change: stone tools, fire, hunting strategies, migration, symbolic behavior, and eventually agriculture and settled life. In that timeline, our species appears not as instantly complete in every sense, but as developing over time through a long process of adaptation and culture.[2][3]

In that sense, the scientific timeline presents humanity as unfolding over time, rather than arriving all at once in a fully articulated form. This slow biological and cultural unfolding raises a deeper question: is the first fully responsible human in the Qur'an's sense the same thing as the first anatomically modern human in the scientific sense?

The Qur’anic timeline of Adam

Classical Muslim sources generally describe Adam as a unique, miraculously created human being, formed from dust or clay and then given a spirit by God, not as the biological child of human parents.[4]

These texts[4] also present Adam as the first in a chain of human beings — “the father of humanity” — from whom later people are called “children of Adam.” Within this framework, many scholars read verses such as “Indeed, the example of Jesus in the sight of Allah is like that of Adam. He created him from dust, then said to him, “Be!” And he was!” (Qur'an 3:59) as implying that Adam, like Jesus, has an origin that cannot be reduced to ordinary biological ancestry.

I am not the first person to ask whether Adam's story might begin inside a much older human species. Muslim scholars such as Shoaib Ahmed Malik have begun to explore this question in detail, arguing that, within a classical Ashʿarī framework, it is possible to accept a long evolutionary history while still holding that Adam's own origin and role are unique.[4]

The bridge

Seen side by side, the scientific and Qur'an's stories are not talking about exactly the same layer of reality. The scientific account follows bodies and cultures across deep time: when Homo sapiens appears in the fossil record, when tools and fire and agriculture emerge. The Qur'an's account begins when a particular human is created, taught, and addressed by God, and when a moral and prophetic history starts. One story asks mainly how a certain kind of creature unfolded; the other asks when a certain kind of responsibility and relationship with the Creator began.

Yet both stories, in their own language, describe a movement from raw material to higher order. Dust becomes stars and planets; dust becomes Adam's body. Life gradually becomes capable of symbolic language and culture; Adam is taught the names and made a vicegerent (khalīfah). For me the question is not which story must erase the other, but whether we can learn to read them together without pretending they are saying exactly the same thing.

What is all of this for?

Once we have two timelines on the table, a deeper question appears: what is all of this for? A universe billions of years old, a species unfolding over hundreds of thousands of years, a prophetic story beginning with Adam — none of this really matters unless we ask what kind of life these stories are inviting us into.

Some people, including many thoughtful agnostics and atheists, answer this by saying there is no built-in purpose at all. The universe just is,[7] life is a temporary pattern in matter, and any meaning we have is something we create for ourselves before we die. From this angle, talking about an afterlife or divine plan feels like a comforting story we tell to avoid facing the finality of death.

The scientific timeline itself doesn't tell us whether there is any purpose beyond survival and whatever meaning we choose to create. It can describe how long stars burn, when Homo sapiens appears, and how civilizations rise and fall, but it stays silent about whether any of this is “for” something.

Contrastingly, the Qur'anic vision, which I was raised with and keep returning to, sees things differently. It speaks of a deliberate creation, of humans being tested, of an afterlife in which hidden realities and intentions are revealed, and a life here as a brief passage in a larger journey. In that view our timeline — whether measured in thousands or billions of years — is not a closed loop but a corridor.

I won't pretend to be neutral here. I do not see life as a movie that simply ends with the credits of death. For me, the idea that we are created, tested, and then brought back to life for a final clarity about our actions makes more sense than the idea that all of this complexity is an accident with no further horizon. But I also know that this is a conviction I hold as a believer; it is not something a microscope can prove or disprove.

From “Who are we?” to “What am I becoming?”

If the Islamic story is right that we are created with a purpose and will be held accountable, then the question “Who are we?” immediately becomes “What kind of person am I becoming?” This is where the neglected language of tazkiyah — purifying the self (soul), not just informing the mind — comes back in. The point is not just to map our timeline, but to shape our character along it.

For me the real value of putting these stories side by side is not to decide which camp we belong to, but to notice what kind of human each story is asking us to become. If there is a Creator and an afterlife, then the timeline is not just information; rather a context for responsibility and inner work. If there is not, then we still have to decide what kind of life is worth living inside a finite universe. Either way, the question returns to the self.

I cannot walk that journey for anyone else. My hope in writing this is simply to offer one pattern that helped me: to see the scientific story as describing how our bodies and world unfolded, and the Qur'anic story as describing when our moral and spiritual story begins — and then ask, very personally, what kind of heart I am bringing to both.

Questions to contemplate

  • Was the universe with its depth and complexities created by chance and without a purpose?
  • What story about the universe and humanity do I actually live by, not just repeat with my mouth?
  • How does that story shape the way I treat knowledge, other people, and my own mistakes?
  • If I discovered tomorrow that some parts of my picture were wrong, am I more attached to being right or to being honest?
